Lucky Pricing: how to price for Chinese-speaking customers

Western retail ends prices in 9. Chinese-market pricing favours 8 and avoids 4 — and research backs it up. A practical guide with examples.

By the 077088 Editorial Team·7 min read·Updated September 2026

The evidence

Lee C. Simmons and Robert M. Schindler studied price advertisements in Chinese markets (Journal of International Marketing, 2003). They found “a distinct tendency to favor the digit 8 and to avoid the digit 4,” and concluded that the pattern gives guidance for setting prices in that market. The explanation is phonetic: 8 (bā) is close to 发 (fā, prosper); 4 (sì) is close to 死 (sǐ, death).

Rules of thumb

  1. Remove every 4, not just the last digit: ¥1,499 still contains 4s.
  2. End in 8 for mass and mid-market: 68, 88, 168, 188, 288, 688.
  3. Use round lucky numbers for premium: 888, 1,688, 2,888, 8,888.
  4. 6 and 9 are good secondary endings: 66 (smooth), 99 (long-lasting).
  5. Love codes are seasonal: 520 and 1314 work on 520 and Qixi, not for a vacuum cleaner in March.
  6. Avoid 250 (二百五 means “fool”).

Beyond prices

The same logic applies to SKU codes, phone numbers on packaging, store addresses, hotel room numbers, and quantities in a bundle (sets of 2, 6, 8 or 9 — never 4). Check any of them with the Number Checker.

When not to bother

If your customers aren't Chinese-speaking, a 4 won't hurt you. For mixed audiences, 8-endings are neutral to Western shoppers and positive to Chinese-speaking ones, so there is little downside.

FAQ

Is 9 good or bad in Chinese pricing?

Good — 九 (jiǔ) sounds like 久 (long-lasting). It's a fine ending, though 8 is stronger.

Should I avoid 4 in the cents?

Yes — $19.40 still shows a 4. Use .88, .68 or .99.
